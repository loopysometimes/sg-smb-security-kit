## Why These Three Attacks Work:

This is not a technical problem, instead it's user error! All three campaigns succeeded not because of sophisticated technical trickery but because they exploited predictable human psychology.

### They seem to target YOU specifically! (they think you have money or something they'd really like!)

They reference institutions every Singapore resident interacts with— IRAS at tax time, Singpass for government services, SingPost for deliveries. The specificity makes them feel legitimate. A generic "your account has been deleted" email raises suspicion. A "your Singpass account linked to your CPF and HDB services will be suspended" email feels real because it knows your world.

**Watch out for:** Emails that reference Singapore-specific services, amounts in SGD, or local contact numbers. Legitimacy of context is not legitimacy of sender.
---

### They manufacture urgency

Every campaign used a deadline:
- IRAS: "refund will be forfeited in 3 days"
- Singpass: "account suspended in 24 hours"
- SingPost: "parcel returned in 48 hours"

Urgency is the attacker's most powerful tool. When you feel time pressure, 
your brain shifts from careful deliberate thinking to fast reactive thinking. 
You stop asking "is this legitimate?" and start asking "how do I fix this fast? before everything explodes in my face!"

**Watch out for:** Any email that tells you to act within 24-72 hours since more often than not, organisations will give you time
---

### They use specific amounts

The IRAS email promised SGD $382.00. The SingPost email charged SGD $50.95.

Both amounts were chosen deliberately. $382 is specific enough to feel 
like a real tax calculation, not a round number someone made up, so does the 50 dollars.

**Watch out for:** Specific dollar amounts in unsolicited emails, 
especially refunds you weren't expecting or fees that seem trivially small.
---

### They impersonate trusted authority

IRAS, Singpass, and SingPost are institutions Singaporeans are conditioned 
to respond to. We pay taxes because IRAS says to. We verify our identity 
because Singpass requires it. We pay fees because SingPost charges them.

Attackers borrow that conditioned trust. The red Singpass logo, the IRAS 
letterhead, the SingPost tracking number; none of it means the email is 
real. It just means the attacker knows what real looks like.

**Watch out for:** Logos and branding are trivially easy to copy. 
Brand recognition is not email authentication. Always verify the 
actual sender address and navigate to official sites directly.
---

### They make victims feel alone

None of these emails said "tell your IT team" or "check with a colleague." 
They addressed the target personally and created a private sense of urgency. 
This is deliberate— the moment you discuss a suspicious email with someone 
else, the spell breaks. A second pair of eyes almost always spots it since they're not under the duress of a running clock.

**Watch out for:** The instinct to handle something quickly and quietly. 
If an email makes you feel like you need to act right now without telling 
anyone, that feeling is the attack.
---

## The Single Most Effective Defence

Before clicking any link in any email that asks you to verify, pay, 
or log in to anything:

**Open a new browser tab and navigate to the official website yourself.**

Type iras.gov.sg. Type singpass.gov.sg. Type singpost.com.sg.

If there is actually a refund, a suspension, or a failed delivery, 
it will be there. If it isn't there, the email was fake.

That one habit stops every campaign documented in this project!!
